## 3. Product overview

DUBBE is an AI dubbing and subtitling system with a website. It takes a video recorded in one language (a lecture, an online course, a blog or a film) and produces a dubbed version in another of 24 languages: it recognizes the speech, translates it, gives each speaker their own voice, keeps the background music and sound effects, places each sentence where it was said, and marks the sentences a person should double-check. The deliverable is a usable draft plus a short review list rather than a black-box video. The pipeline in short is: video, speech recognition with word timestamps, sentence segmentation, translation, speech synthesis, timing, dubbed video.

Each DUBBE run produces a dubbed MP4 with the new voice track, a segments.json file with every sentence's source text, translation, timestamps, confidence values, timing data and flags, a report.html page listing the sentences to check and why, a stats.json file with run time and cost, and subtitle files in SRT, WebVTT and ASS formats. All AI output is editable: a corrected sentence is re-synthesized either from the command line or with the website's Re-dub button.

DUBBE is offered to users through a website with nine services: Dubbing, Text to Speech, Speech to Text, Speaker Detection, Subtitles, Translate, Voice Isolator, Voice Cloning and a Voice Marketplace. Every service runs the pipeline's own stages as a queued GPU job, and the dubbing service includes a review editor with a coloured timeline of flagged sentences.

## 4. Problem

The problem DUBBE addresses is that video is recorded in one language while many of the people who need it do not speak that language well. Korean universities record lectures in Korean while international enrollment grows, online course creators can only sell in the language they filmed in, and bloggers and TV studios want to reach audiences in other languages. Useful video therefore reaches only a fraction of its intended audience.

The existing options do not close this gap. Subtitles compete with the screen, so viewers cannot read and follow a diagram at the same time. Professional dubbing with translators and voice actors is accurate but priced per minute, so it is rarely justified for large amounts of routine material. Automated dubbing tools such as YouTube auto-dub, ElevenLabs, HeyGen, Rask and Perso are cheap but return a finished file with no indication of which sentences are wrong. In a chained pipeline errors compound: a misheard word becomes a confident mistranslation and then fluent synthetic speech that says something wrong and sounds trustworthy. Checking a dub by ear takes as long as the video itself, per language, and needs someone who knows both languages, so most people either do not check or do not dub at all.

DUBBE needs AI because every step of dubbing (understanding speech, translating meaning, producing a natural voice, telling speakers apart, hearing emotion) depends on models rather than rules or a database lookup. The same models also report how sure they are, and DUBBE turns these confidence signals into the review list that tells a person where to look.

## 5. Users and market

The buyer chosen for the capstone is online course creators and small edtech companies, who want to sell into new language markets, decide quickly and cannot afford professional dubbing at all. Adjacent segments, in order of later interest, are universities with international students and large archives of Korean lectures, corporate training departments at multinationals, and SMEs or public agencies producing training and information videos for foreign workers and residents.

DUBBE's wider customer groups are content creators and bloggers who want audiences in other languages (B2C), online course authors, training centres and universities that publish courses on their own platforms (B2C and B2B), and TV channels and dubbing studios that dub films and series for local audiences (B2B), where DUBBE would produce the first draft and the studio's staff would check only the flagged sentences. The first users the team can reach directly are professors and students at Inha University.

The DUBBE documents contain no sourced market-size figure yet. The market signals they record are that AI dubbing has become common and cheap: AI dubbing tools charge about 0.10 to 3 US dollars per dubbed minute, and YouTube dubs videos on its own platform for free, with over 6 million daily viewers watching dubbed content in December 2025 according to YouTube. Professional human dubbing is quoted between about 15 and 250 dollars per minute in the team's sources, none of them verified.

## 6. Competition

DUBBE's main competitors are ElevenLabs (94 languages, voice cloning, Dubbing Studio editing, no lip sync, about 0.50 to 0.60 dollars per minute automatic and 1.65 to 2.00 in Dubbing Studio), HeyGen (175+ languages, lip sync, an edit and review step, about 0.10 to 0.25 dollars per minute by credits), Rask AI (130+ languages, transcript editor, lip sync on higher tiers, about 2 to 3 dollars per minute), Perso AI by the Korean company ESTsoft (33+ languages, lip sync on paid plans, 0.55 to 1.00 dollars per minute), CAMB.AI and Deepdub for film and TV, YouTube auto-dub (free but only for videos published on YouTube, 27 languages, lip sync only as a pilot), the open-source pyVideoTrans (runs offline, no review flags) and traditional dubbing studios working with actors. All the commercial self-serve tools run only in the cloud.

DUBBE differs from its competitors in five ways. It marks per sentence which parts of the dub may be wrong, which none of the self-serve dubbing tools the team checked does; segment-level quality scores exist in text translation tools such as Smartling and Lokalise but not in self-serve dubbing. It can run on the customer's own machine or server, so unreleased films and private lectures are never uploaded. It is built from open models, so each stage can be replaced or trained on new data such as Uzbek. Every step is editable, and a corrected sentence is re-dubbed. It can slow the picture slightly where a translation is longer, which no competitor the team checked advertises.

DUBBE is behind its competitors in voice naturalness, lip sync (not offered), number of languages (24 against up to 175+) and, above all, users: competitors have customers and DUBBE has none yet.

## 7. Business model, pricing and unit economics

DUBBE's proposed consumer pricing, shown on the website but not yet tested with customers, has three plans: Free at 0 dollars for 15 minutes of video per month (videos up to 10 minutes, kept 7 days), Creator at 9 dollars per month for 120 minutes (videos up to 60 minutes, kept 30 days), and Pro at 29 dollars per month for 600 minutes (videos up to 180 minutes, kept 90 days). During the beta everyone is on the Free plan and the paid plans are a waitlist.

DUBBE's business customers (studios, TV channels, universities) would buy per-minute or per-project contracts, or a licence to run DUBBE on their own server so that their content never leaves their premises.

DUBBE's unit economics: the Creator plan sells a video minute for about 0.075 dollars and the Pro plan for about 0.048 dollars, while the measured GPU cost is 0.003 to 0.007 dollars per video minute. Servers, storage and support are additional costs that are not yet calculated. DUBBE has no users and no revenue so far.

Before DUBBE can charge money, its translation model must be replaced, because NLLB-200 is licensed CC-BY-NC 4.0 for non-commercial use only. The website therefore states that DUBBE is non-commercial during the beta. Other translation models have been measured as candidates; MADLAD-400 3B scored close to NLLB on the Korean–English test set.

## 8. Operating costs

DUBBE currently costs almost nothing to run: it works on the team's own laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 4060 (8 GB), all models are free and open, and no one is paid because all team members are students. Electricity on the laptop is at most about 0.0001 to 0.0004 dollars per video minute. DUBBE needs about 39 GB of disk space: 25 GB of model weights, 0.4 GB of Torch cache, 8.4 GB for the main Python environment, 5.4 GB for the Chatterbox environment and 0.1 GB of voice samples.

Three hosting setups have been costed for DUBBE with October 2026 prices. Setup A, a pilot served from the laptop through a free Cloudflare Tunnel, costs about 3 to 13 dollars per month (a .com domain at 10.46 dollars per year, free email up to 3,000 messages per month with Resend, electricity 2 to 12 dollars), but the site is offline whenever the laptop is off. Setup B, an always-on rented RTX 4090 on RunPod at 0.34 dollars per hour plus a 100 GB disk, costs about 260 dollars per month, or about 515 dollars on RunPod's more reliable Secure tier. Setup C, a small web server with the GPU started only while dubbing, costs about 16 to 20 dollars per month for 1,000 video minutes (server about 5 dollars, storage 7 dollars, GPU 0.003 to 0.007 dollars per minute), but the code that starts and stops the GPU has not been written.

DUBBE's planned one-time cost is training models for Uzbek and better quality on a GPU rented by the hour: about 100 to 200 GPU hours, estimated at 50 to 300 dollars in total.

## 10. Risks

DUBBE's main risks and responses are these. The translation model is non-commercial, so it will be replaced before any payment is taken. No native speaker has rated the output, so listeners will rate each language, starting with Uzbek, Russian and English. The flags miss confident errors, so translation with the surrounding conversation and an extra quality model (COMET or an LLM judge) are planned. Uzbek is not supported, so Uzbek will be measured and an Uzbek voice trained. Large competitors such as ElevenLabs and YouTube are answered with the review list, on-premise operation and local languages and studios. Misuse of voices is limited by consent-only cloning, encrypted recordings, never publishing voices taken from videos, and a page for reporting misuse. Because DUBBE started as a university course project, ownership of the code must be agreed in writing with the course instructor.

## 11. Architecture

DUBBE's core is a Python package (src/dubbe) with one module per pipeline stage and a command-line entry point (cli.py, command dubbe). All language settings live in one configuration file, languages.yaml, so adding a language pair means adding one entry. Each run writes its intermediate files into a work folder named after the video and the language pair (work/video.src-tgt/), so any stage can be inspected, edited or re-run on its own.

DUBBE's website (src/dubbe/web) adds FastAPI, SQLite and Jinja HTML templates on top of the pipeline, with one CSS file and plain JavaScript. The main modules are app.py (routes and pages), auth.py (accounts and sessions), db.py (the SQLite database with tables for users, sessions, tokens, projects, jobs, messages, tasks, voices, voice uses, voice saves and voice reviews), tools.py (the single-purpose tools), clones.py (voice cloning) and worker.py. The worker runs waiting jobs one at a time by calling the normal dubbe command in a subprocess, so the website runs exactly the same code as the command line and all GPU memory is freed after each job; the current stage is shown as progress. Data, uploads and outputs live in data/web, which is git-ignored.

DUBBE runs the Chatterbox voice models in a separate Python environment (.venv-chatterbox) because Chatterbox pins Torch 2.6 and NumPy below 2, while the main environment uses Torch 2.8. The pipeline talks to it through chatterbox_worker.py. If that environment is missing, Korean falls back to the MMS-TTS voice and English vocal sounds are reported as not performed.

DUBBE's website is configured through environment variables in a git-ignored .env file: DUBBE_HOST, DUBBE_PORT and DUBBE_SITE_URL (where the site listens and the address used in emails), DUBBE_WEB_DATA (the data folder), DUBBE_ADMINS (emails with admin rights and no plan limits), DUBBE_TEAM_EMAIL (where contact messages go), DUBBE_WORKER (1 runs the dub worker inside the web process), SMTP_HOST, SMTP_PORT, SMTP_USER, SMTP_PASSWORD and DUBBE_MAIL_FROM (email sending; without SMTP, mails are written to data/web/outbox), and DUBBE_VOICE_KEY (the key that encrypts voice recordings; if it is lost, recordings cannot be read again).

## 12. Pipeline stages

The first seven DUBBE pipeline stages are: 1 extract, where ffmpeg produces a 16 kHz mono audio file for recognition and the original stereo audio; 2 separate, where Demucs (htdemucs) splits the original into a voice stem and a background stem, and the background is dropped when it is more than 35 dB below the original because it is then only separation residue; 3 asr, where faster-whisper transcribes the original mix with word timestamps and drops words over silence quieter than −60 dB, because Whisper invents text over silence and reads the original mix better than the separated voice (9.5% versus 19.8% character errors); 4 segment, which groups words into sentences using sentence endings and pauses (a 0.3 s pause after an ending closes a sentence, 0.8 s closes one on its own, sentences longer than 15 s or 40 words are split, fragments under 1 s are merged); 5 speakers, which detects who speaks each sentence; 6 emotion, which adds delivery tags heard in the original; and 7 voice, which chooses a preset voice per speaker.

The remaining DUBBE pipeline stages are: 8 translate, where NLLB-200 3.3B with 8 beams produces each sentence's translation plus 4 alternatives and a confidence value; 9 tts, which synthesizes each sentence with Kokoro (English), Chatterbox Turbo (expressive English or English clones) or Chatterbox Multilingual (all other languages); 10 shorten, which replaces a translation that is too long for its time slot with a shorter alternative that keeps at least 85% of the best confidence and re-synthesizes it; 11 timing, which anchors every sentence to its source start time, speeds speech up by at most 1.3 times, optionally slows the picture, matches loudness and follows the original's delivery; 12 mux, where ffmpeg mixes the new voice with the background (ducked under the voice) and writes the video, re-encoding it only if the picture was slowed; and 13 report, which computes the flags, the cost and report.html.

DUBBE is run with the command dubbe followed by the video file and options: --src and --tgt set the source and target languages (Korean and English by default), --work sets the folder for intermediate files, --from re-runs the pipeline from a given stage (for example after editing segments.json), --force re-runs every stage, --slow-video allows slowing the picture up to a factor such as 1.2, --no-background keeps only the voice and skips separation, and --voice chooses the dub's voice (a preset of the target language or a clone). To correct a translation, the reviewer edits the target text in segments.json, marks the sentence as edited and runs the command again with --from tts.

A per-stage timing example from one DUBBE run of the 2 minute 43 second FLEURS Korean lecture dubbed into English on the RTX 4060: extract 0.1 s, separate 11.2 s, asr 29.5 s, segment 0.0 s, speakers 12.8 s, emotion 6.9 s, voice 0.4 s, translate 15.0 s, tts 19.8 s, shorten 0.1 s, timing 1.9 s, mux 2.8 s and report 0.1 s, about 100 s in total.

## 13. Data formats

DUBBE's main data file is segments.json. At the top level it stores the source and target languages, the detected speakers (with names and chosen voices), the voice engine that was used and the dub's gain, plus the list of segments. Each segment records an id, start and end times, the speaker, the source text and the target text, the alternative translations (alts), the speech recognition confidence (asr_conf), the translation confidence (mt_conf) and its token count, whether it was shortened or edited, the delivery tags chosen by the reviewer (tags), the tags heard automatically (auto_tags), tags the voice could not perform, the original's relative loudness and pitch (delivery_db, delivery_st), the synthesized duration (tts_dur), the placed start and output start, the speed-up (stretch), the picture slow-down (slow), the overflow into the next sentence, and the review flag.

Besides segments.json, each DUBBE work folder contains audio.wav, original.wav, vocals.wav and background.wav (the extracted and separated audio), words.json (recognized words with timestamps), a tts folder with one audio file per sentence, dub.wav (the new voice track), dubbed.mp4, report.html, run.json (the time each stage took, the input video and the options), stats.json (run time and cost) and ratings.json when sentences were rated. The cost assumptions are kept in languages.yaml: 115 W GPU power, 0.15 dollars per kWh, 0.35 dollars per hour for a cloud GPU, and the ElevenLabs range of 0.33 to 2.20 dollars per minute from the project tab.

## 14. Models and licences

DUBBE uses only open models that run locally. Speech recognition uses Whisper large-v3 or large-v3-turbo, chosen per language, through faster-whisper (MIT). Voice and background separation uses Demucs htdemucs (MIT). Translation uses NLLB-200 3.3B converted to CTranslate2 with int8 weights (CC-BY-NC 4.0, non-commercial). The English voice is Kokoro-82M (Apache 2.0). Voices in the other languages come from Chatterbox Multilingual (MIT), and the expressive English voice from Chatterbox Turbo (MIT). Speaker detection uses WavLM base plus SV (no licence on the model card; the WavLM code is MIT). Emotion detection uses HuBERT large fine-tuned on SUPERB emotion recognition (Apache 2.0), and sounds such as laughing and coughing are detected with an AST AudioSet classifier (BSD 3-Clause). The stages run one at a time, so each can use the whole 8 GB GPU.

DUBBE's model choices changed as tests came in. WhisperX was evaluated and rejected because its alignment found only 3 of 11 pauses on Korean speech, so faster-whisper's own word timestamps are used. Korean recognition moved to Whisper large-v3 (5.3% character errors on read speech against 7.7% for turbo), while turbo stays where large-v3 was not better. Translation moved on 16 October 2026 from NLLB-200 distilled 600M to NLLB-200 3.3B after five models were compared on 529 Korean–English sentence pairs. The Korean voice moved from Meta MMS-TTS (CC-BY-NC, flat and robotic) to Chatterbox Multilingual (MIT) on 15 October 2026, which cut Whisper round-trip errors from 15.3% to 4.1%. Writing Korean numbers out as words before speaking cut errors on sentences with digits from 10.7% to 1.7%.

## 15. Languages, voices and delivery

DUBBE supports 24 languages in any pair: Korean, English, Arabic, Danish, German, Greek, Spanish, Finnish, French, Hindi, Italian, Japanese, Malay, Dutch, Norwegian, Polish, Portuguese, Russian, Swedish, Swahili, Turkish, Chinese, Uzbek and Kazakh. Each language's entry in languages.yaml gives its display name, its Whisper language code and recognition model, its NLLB language code, its sentence-ending pattern and its voice engine and voices. Hebrew was left out because recognition reached 29% errors and voices 38 to 47%, and Uzbek is not supported yet.

DUBBE's voices: English uses 26 Kokoro preset voices. The other languages use Chatterbox Multilingual's built-in voice, and the same 26 English presets can speak every other language by giving Chatterbox the preset's English reading as a voice reference; in Korean, 20 of the 26 presets are understood at 2.3% character errors. The built-in voice was dropped for French, Hindi and Swahili, where it reached 66%, 20% and 29% errors, so English presets speak those languages. A check called cut_babble has Whisper listen to each Chatterbox sentence and cuts any babble after the last word. The website offers 590 voice samples across the 24 languages.

DUBBE chooses a preset voice for each speaker by the speaker's pitch register: speakers below about 122 Hz get a lower voice and those above get a higher one, which picked the right register for 95% of sentences on held-out FLEURS speech. The reviewer can override the choice for any speaker, and the override survives re-runs.

DUBBE supports delivery tags per sentence, such as [slow], [fast], [soft], [loud], [pause], [question], [whisper], [excited], [sad], [angry], [laugh], [sigh], [gasp] and [cough]. Tags heard in the original are added automatically and shown dashed in the editor: emotions when the HuBERT emotion model is at least 0.9 sure (82% of its tags right on the CREMA-D test set, finding 34% of emotional sentences), sounds when the AudioSet classifier reaches 0.3, and questions when the source ends with a question mark. Vocal sounds such as laughing are only performed in English through Chatterbox Turbo. In addition, every dubbed sentence follows the original's loudness (within 6 dB) and part of its pitch movement (within 1.5 semitones), with short fades and a soft limiter.

## 16. Review flags and quality measurement

DUBBE flags a sentence for review from three signals: the speech recognition confidence (mean word probability below 0.8), the translation confidence (mean token probability below 0.6 for English targets and below 0.5 for Korean targets) and timing overflow (more than 0.05 s past the next sentence's start). The flags appear as coloured blocks on the video timeline: translation, speech recognition, timing, or OK. The thresholds were calibrated so that about a third of sentences are flagged (34% for Korean to English, 41% for English to Korean).

DUBBE's review flags are useful but incomplete. In the first informal test on 15 FLEURS sentences, 3 sentences were flagged and 2 of them were really wrong, but about 8 sentences were wrong by the author's reading, so the flags missed about 6 confident errors (for example Ghana instead of Canaan). After recalibration, flagged sentences are clearly worse than unflagged ones (chrF++ 27 against 37 for English to Korean). The time the flags save a reviewer has not been measured yet.

DUBBE includes tools to measure quality. The editor lets a reviewer rate each sentence from 0 to 100 and pick what is wrong (wrong meaning, wrong word, missing words, mispronounced, cut off, bad timing), with an evaluation mode that hides the flags while rating, and a script summarizes scores, flag precision and recall, and agreement between raters. An error-propagation script compares each stage against a reference to show which stage causes the errors; on FLEURS it showed that speech recognition costs 10.8 chrF points (49.4 rising to 60.1 with the correct transcript).

## 17. Timing

DUBBE keeps the dub in sync by anchoring every sentence to the start time of the original sentence, so errors never accumulate over a long video. When a translation is longer than its slot, DUBBE first speeds the speech up by at most 1.3 times, then uses a shorter alternative translation, and, if allowed with --slow-video, slows the picture slightly where needed; anything left over spills into the following pause instead of shifting later sentences. On the test clips the largest delay was 0.00 s, and in the long-translation test the maximum speed-up fell from 1.30 to 1.18 times and overflowing sentences from 16 to 0, at the cost of 4.5% more video length.

## 18. Speakers and voice cloning

DUBBE detects speakers by computing a WavLM voice fingerprint for every sentence and clustering the sentences (average linkage, cosine distance threshold 0.3). Two different voices were separated in 100% of sentences, four mixed voices in 88%, and a real two-person dialogue on the website had 13 of 13 sentences on the right speaker, detected in 27 s. Very similar voices merge: three similar women stayed one speaker, and a Breaking Bad scene with two men was merged into one, so the reviewer can rename speakers, merge them, or split a sentence off as a new speaker.

DUBBE lets users clone their own voice, which the instructor approved on 15 October 2026 although the course originally ruled cloning out. The user records sentences picked at that moment, Whisper checks that they were read, the recording is stored encrypted, and dubs made with a clone carry a watermark and an AI label. A clone reaches a speaker similarity of 0.96 in English and 0.89 in Korean, against 0.52 to 0.60 for built-in voices. A speaker from an uploaded video can be cloned only with the uploader's consent; that voice stays private inside the project, cannot be published, and reached a similarity of 0.85 against 0.66 for a stock voice. Tests use synthetic voices only, never real people's voices.

DUBBE's voice marketplace lets owners share their own cloned voices: an opt-in listing goes through admin review, other users can search, rate, review and get a voice to use it, and a Voice Studio shows publishers their progress, listing editor, uses, rating and what they would earn at 0.03 dollars per 1,000 characters. Payouts are not built, and the instructor has not yet confirmed that the marketplace counts as business scope.

## 19. Subtitles

DUBBE's subtitle service colours captions by speaker in the BBC subtitle colour order (white, yellow, cyan, green, then magenta, orange, blue and red) and exports them as SRT with font colours, as WebVTT with voice tags and a style block, or as ASS with one style per speaker. Captions can be burned into the video with ffmpeg and libx264, which takes about 6 seconds for a 36 second video; NVIDIA hardware encoding is not used because the laptop's driver is too old. Fonts were checked for Korean, Japanese, Chinese, Arabic and Hindi. Renaming a speaker rewrites every caption file, and a finished dub can also be turned into a video with captions.

## 22. Setup, hardware and commands

DUBBE requires ffmpeg on the PATH, Python 3.10 to 3.12 (Kokoro needs a version below 3.13) and preferably an NVIDIA GPU; it also runs on a CPU, more slowly. It was developed on a laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 4060 (8 GB) under Windows. Installation creates a virtual environment, installs CUDA PyTorch 2.8 separately, and then installs the package with the extras models (faster-whisper 1.2.1, transformers, sentencepiece, Kokoro 0.9.4, uroman, Demucs 4.1.0), gui (Gradio), web (FastAPI, Uvicorn, Jinja2, python-multipart, cryptography), eval (sacrebleu) and dev (pytest). The first run downloads about 5 GB of model weights, and Chatterbox 0.1.7 is installed in its own .venv-chatterbox with Torch 2.6.

DUBBE's commands are: dubbe lecture.mp4 --src ko --tgt en, which writes work/lecture.ko-en/dubbed.mp4 and report.html; dubbe-web, which starts the website; dubbe-gui, which starts the Gradio page; pytest, which runs the unit tests; scripts/make_voice_samples.py, which creates the website's voice samples; scripts/make_fleurs_clip.py, which builds a Korean test clip with reference text; scripts/drift_check.py (timing drift, test T-03), scripts/background_check.py (background kept, T-12) and scripts/voice_check.py (voice register, T-14); scripts/ratings_summary.py for rating statistics; scripts/eval_propagation.py for the error-propagation study; and scripts/create_user.py for accounts.

## 23. Testing and measured results

DUBBE's measured Korean–English results: Whisper large-v3 makes 5.3% character errors on read Korean speech (100 FLEURS sentences) and 11.7% on conversational Korean (150 KsponSpeech clips), most of the latter being spelling variants. NLLB-200 3.3B scores chrF++ 52.3 from Korean to English and 32.8 from English to Korean on 529 sentence pairs with human references, up from 49.3 and 29.4 with the 600M model; on the FLEURS lecture re-run on the website, translation rose from 54.1 to 58.6. Short spoken lines are still sometimes mistranslated because each sentence is translated without the conversation around it.

DUBBE's measured results in the other 20 languages (FLEURS read speech; recognition character errors, then chrF++ from English and into English): Arabic 4.4%, 53 and 65; Danish 3.2%, 68 and 71; German 1.8%, 63 and 70; Greek 3.2%, 52 and 62; Spanish 2.4%, 55 and 60; Finnish 4.5%, 52 and 60; French 4.1%, 70 and 68; Hindi 13.0%, 58 and 67; Italian 1.4%, 59 and 64; Japanese 6.8%, 24 and 56; Malay 10.1%, 66 and 68; Dutch 3.2%, 54 and 58; Norwegian 3.6%, 57 and 65; Polish 2.4%, 47 and 57; Portuguese 2.2%, 69 and 72; Russian 3.8%, 56 and 64; Swedish 2.9%, 68 and 72; Swahili 26.3%, 60 and 67; Turkish 17.2%, 58 and 65; Chinese 8.8%, 23 and 55. chrF++ is not comparable for Japanese and Chinese because they have no spaces between words. Full dubs through the pipeline: English to French 2.1% errors after the babble cut, Spanish to English 0.5%.

DUBBE's measured speed and cost: about 29 seconds of processing per minute of video into English and 77 seconds into Korean on the RTX 4060, which at an assumed 0.35 dollars per GPU hour is about 0.003 dollars per video minute into English and 0.007 into Korean. The background test kept a phone ring at −0.0 dB against the original with 0 words of the original voice leaking into the background, and music stays 11.5 dB under the voice, as in the original.
