How DUBBE works

Thirteen steps run on a GPU, one after another. Each step saves its result, so a correction re-runs only what comes after it.

{{ pipeline() }}

The steps

  1. Extract the sound from the video.
  2. Separate voice from background (Demucs), so music and effects can stay under the new voice.
  3. Recognise speech with word timestamps (Whisper large-v3 or large-v3-turbo via faster-whisper — whichever made fewer errors in that language in our tests).
  4. Split into sentences by punctuation and pauses.
  5. Tell speakers apart: a voice fingerprint per sentence (WavLM), sentences grouped by voice.
  6. Hear the delivery: questions, emotion (HuBERT) and sounds such as laughs and sighs (AST) in the original become tags on the sentence.
  7. Pick a voice for each speaker that matches their pitch register — a preset voice, a clone of your own voice, or, only if you confirm they agreed, the speaker's own voice cloned from the video.
  8. Translate each sentence (NLLB-200 3.3B), keeping several alternative wordings and a confidence for each.
  9. Speak the translation: Kokoro for English; Chatterbox Multilingual for the other languages, with its built-in voice or an English preset speaking the language. When the voice runs on past the sentence, speech recognition finds the end and cuts there.
  10. Shorten: if a sentence is too long for its slot, use a shorter alternative wording that is almost as confident.
  11. Time each sentence to where it was said: speed up at most 1.3×, optionally slow the picture slightly.
  12. Mix the new voice with the background and write the video, labelled AI-generated.
  13. Report: flags, timeline, processing time and cost.

What the colours mean

translation the translator was unsure of this sentence · speech recognition the recogniser was unsure what was said · timing the dub does not fit its slot even after speeding up · OK no warning.

Flags are a short list, not a guarantee. On our first test they caught 2 real errors among 3 flagged sentences, but missed translations that were wrong and confident (for example a wrong place name). Listen to the whole dub before publishing it. Details

Timing without drift

Every sentence starts where the original sentence started, so small differences never add up over a long video. When a translation is longer than the original, DUBBE first uses a shorter wording, then speeds the voice up (at most 1.3×), and — only if you allow it — slows the picture a little (up to 1.3×).

Background sound

Music, effects and other sounds are separated from the speaker's voice and mixed back under the dub; the background is lowered only while the new voice speaks. In our test, a phone ringing between sentences kept its original level (−0.0 dB) with no words of the original voice left over.

Vocal tags such as [laugh] or [sigh] need an expressive voice; DUBBE marks them "not performed" when the voice cannot do them. Tags DUBBE heard in the original are shown dashed; untick the wrong ones. Corrected sentences are never re-translated. A re-dub runs only the voice, timing and mixing steps again, and uses no plan minutes. Transcribe & translate again re-runs a project with the newest models and keeps your corrections.

Several speakers

DUBBE compares the voice of every sentence and groups the sentences by speaker; the main speaker is the one who talks most. Each speaker gets a different voice that matches their pitch, and the transcript and subtitles can name them. In the editor you rename speakers, merge two that are one person, or move a sentence to another speaker — or to a new one. In our tests a man and a woman were told apart without a mistake, but two similar men were taken for one person, so check the speaker chips.

A speaker's own voice can be cloned from the video only after you confirm that person agreed. The copy is stored encrypted inside the project and deleted with it; from Speaker Detection you can instead save it as a private voice, which can never be published. Speaker Detection also works on its own: upload a video or audio and get who speaks when, with a transcript by speaker.

Subtitles in colour

Captions can give each speaker their own colour — white for the main speaker, then yellow, cyan and green, the order of the BBC subtitle guidelines — as SRT, WebVTT and ASS, and burned into the video when you ask. The Subtitles tool does this for any video, with an optional translation; a dub's download page does it for the dub.
